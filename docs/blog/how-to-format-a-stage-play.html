There is no official stage play format, but there is a conventional layout that most theatres and competitions expect, and a reader can tell within a page whether you know it. This guide goes through that layout one element at a time, with a sample scene, and ends with a free template you can download or open in your browser.

The format at a glance

Here is one page of a correctly formatted scene. Most of the elements covered below appear in it.

                         ACT ONE

                        Scene One

  The back garden of a large country house. Late
  afternoon, summer. A long table is set for tea.
  HELEN stands at the table, arranging flowers.

                          HELEN
          Four o'clock. They'll be here any minute.

          (RUTH enters from the house, carrying a
          teapot.)

                          RUTH
          You've rearranged the table three times.
          It's a garden party, not a state dinner.

                          HELEN
                        (not looking up)
          It's the first time James has been home in
          two years. I want it to feel right.

          (RUTH sets the teapot down and exits into
          the house.)

Screenplay format is rigid because production departments schedule and budget from it line by line. Stage play format is looser. Its job is to be easy to read in a literary office and easy to use in a rehearsal room, and most of the conventions below exist for one of those two readers.

Title page

The title page carries the title, the form, your name and your contact details:

  • The title, centred in capitals, roughly a third of the way down the page.
  • The form, one line below: a play in one act, a play in two acts, a monologue.
  • by on its own line, then your name.
  • Contact details in a lower corner: name, email, and optionally phone and agent. If the play is registered or copyrighted, a single line here is enough.

A draft date in the other lower corner is optional, and some theatres ask for one so they know which version they are reading. Leave off loglines and artwork. Competitions that read blind will ask you to remove the contact block entirely, so keep it in one place where it is easy to strip out.

Character list

The next page lists the characters, headed CHARACTERS or CAST. List every speaking character in capitals, in order of appearance or in order of importance, with a one-line description:

                        CHARACTERS

HELEN      60s. Owner of the house. Has been planning
           this afternoon for a month.
RUTH       60s. Helen's sister. Lives in the village
           and visits daily.
JAMES      30s. Helen's son. Has not been home in two
           years.

Give an age range, the character's relationship to the others, and one detail that matters to the story. Skip appearance unless the plot depends on it, and keep biographies out. A character with no lines is still listed, with the note (non-speaking).

Below the list, two short italic notes: Setting, where the play takes place in one sentence, and Time, when. If the play has one location and runs in real time, say so here.

Act and scene headings

Act headings are centred in capitals and numbered in words or Roman numerals: ACT ONE or ACT I. Both are standard. Arabic digits for acts, as in Act 2, are the one form that reads as unfamiliar with the convention. Each act starts on a new page.

Scene headings are centred in title case below the act heading: Scene One or Scene 1. Published acting editions use both. Scenes can run on from each other or start a new page, as long as the script is consistent about it.

Scene titles are uncommon in stage plays. If you use them, put the title on the line below the number or after a dash. There is no fixed rule beyond consistency. The Scriptwriter sets them after an em dash, Scene Two — The Return, which is one reasonable way to do it.

Setting lines

Every scene opens with a description of what the audience sees and hears when the lights come up, usually in italic at the left margin:

  A kitchen in a small flat. Early morning. A table,
  two chairs, a window looking onto the street. ALEX
  stands at the window.

This is where a little description belongs. Give the designer the essentials: place, time of day, season if it matters, the furniture the action needs, and who is on stage at the top. Leave out colour schemes, wallpaper and anything the audience cannot see. If the setting is the same as the previous scene, say The same. That evening. and move on.

Character names and dialogue

The speaking character's name is centred, in capitals, on its own line. The dialogue sits below it, indented from the left margin, in normal sentence case. The name and the first line of dialogue should never be separated by a page break.

                          ALEX
          Four o'clock. They'll be here any minute.

                         JORDAN
          You've rearranged the table three times.

When a speech runs over a page break, repeat the name at the top of the next page with (cont.) or (continued). When a stage direction interrupts a speech, you can either set the direction inside the speech as an indented line and carry on without the name, or repeat the name, in which case most writers add (cont.) there too. The Scriptwriter adds it automatically when the same character speaks twice in a row.

For two characters speaking the same words at once, use BOTH, or both names on one line: ALEX AND JORDAN. For overlapping but different lines, many writers set the two speeches side by side in columns. If you need to stress a word, use italics, not bold or capitals, and do it rarely. Rhythm and word order usually carry the emphasis better.

Stage directions and parentheticals

Stage play format has two kinds of direction, and readers notice when a writer confuses them.

Stage directions

Physical action between speeches. Set in parentheses, usually italic, indented to the dialogue margin or a little further:

          (JORDAN puts the suitcase down and does not
          sit.)

Write what happens, not what a character feels. (JORDAN hesitates) gives the actor something to play. (JORDAN, overwhelmed by the memory of the last time they stood in this kitchen, hesitates) is a paragraph from a novel: the memory behind the hesitation is the actor's business, not yours. Stage directions are written in the present tense.

Parentheticals

A short note on delivery, in parentheses, between the character name and the line:

                         JORDAN
                        (quietly)
          I'm not staying.

Use them sparingly. If a line can only land one way, the problem is usually the line. Experienced playwrights go pages without one.

Cues: entrances, exits, sound and light

Entrances, exits and effects are what a stage manager marks up first in the prompt copy, so they need to be easy to find. Three habits help:

  • Capitalise the character's name on every entrance and exit: (ALEX exits through the door to the street.)
  • Give each entrance and exit its own line rather than burying it inside a paragraph of business.
  • Write sound and light as plain stage directions: (Lights fade.), (A train passes, close.) You do not need the terms SFX or LX in a submission draft; the production will add them.

A useful check before you send a draft: read only the cues and nothing else. Anyone who exits must have come on, and anyone still on stage at the end of a scene must have been put there by the setting paragraph or an entrance. This is tedious by hand, which is why The Scriptwriter tracks entrances and exits per scene, shows who is on stage at any line, and will not let you move a speech to a point where its speaker has not yet entered.

Font, margins and page numbers

ElementStandard
Font12-point Times New Roman or Courier. Any plain serif at 12 point is accepted. Avoid anything decorative.
PaperUS Letter in North America, A4 in the UK and Europe. Most submissions are now PDF, so set the page size once and export.
MarginsOne inch all round, with 1.5 inches on the binding side if the script will be bound. This is the Dramatists Guild's published guideline.
Line spacingSingle within a speech. One blank line between elements.
Page numbersTop right, starting from the first page of the script. The title page is not numbered. Some theatres use act-scene-page numbering (I-2-14) so pages can be replaced in rehearsal; follow their guidelines if they publish any.
Page lengthA page runs roughly a minute on stage, often a little more. Treat it as an estimate and time a read-aloud to be sure. A full-length play usually lands between about 70 and 120 pages; a one-act anywhere from 10 to 60. Competitions define these by running time, so check their rules.

The page-a-minute estimate only holds in standard format. Shrinking the font or margins to hit a page count is obvious on the page and gets found out at the first read-through.

US and UK conventions

Everything above follows the US manuscript convention, which broadly matches the sample formats the Dramatists Guild publishes and is what most American theatres mean by "standard format". The UK has a parallel tradition with two visible differences:

  • Character names are often set in capitals at the left margin with the dialogue alongside, rather than centred above the speech. This is the layout of the Samuel French acting editions and of many UK submission manuscripts.
  • Stage directions are set in italic, and most UK publishers leave off the parentheses.

Neither is more correct. A UK theatre will read a US-format script without complaint and vice versa. What matters is consistency within the script and following any explicit guidelines a theatre or competition publishes. If they publish none, either layout is safe. This guide uses the US one.

Common mistakes

The ones literary departments see most often:

  1. Screenplay format. INT. KITCHEN — DAY sluglines, CUT TO transitions, camera directions. If you wrote a first draft in screenwriting software, strip these out before anyone sees it.
  2. Novelistic stage directions. Thoughts, memories and backstory belong in the dialogue or nowhere. The stage direction describes what the audience sees.
  3. Inconsistent character names. ALEX on one page, Alex on the next, ALEXANDER in Act Two. Pick one spelling and use it everywhere, including the character list.
  4. Missing entrances. A character speaks in a scene without being placed there by the setting paragraph or an entrance. Readers hit this and stop to work out who is in the room.
  5. Arabic digits for acts. Act 2. Use words or Roman numerals.
  6. A parenthetical on every line. If every speech needs a delivery note, the dialogue is doing less work than it should.
  7. Non-standard page setup used to hit a page count. Cut the play, not the margins.

Set the format up once in a template and stop thinking about it. The less a reader notices the layout, the more they notice the play.
