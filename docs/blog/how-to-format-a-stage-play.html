There is no single official stage play format, but there is a standard that every theatre, competition and literary manager will recognise on sight. This guide walks through it one element at a time, with a worked example, and ends with a free template you can download or open straight in your browser.

The format at a glance

Here is one page of a correctly formatted stage play. Every element in this guide appears in it.

                         ACT ONE

                        Scene One

  A kitchen in a small flat. Early morning. A cup
  of coffee goes cold on the counter.

          (ALEX stands at the window.)

          (JORDAN enters from the hallway, carrying a
          suitcase.)

                          ALEX
          Dialogue goes here. Keep every line
          speakable. Read it aloud.

                         JORDAN
                        (quietly)
          A parenthetical sits between the name and
          the line.

          (ALEX exits through the door to the street.)

Unlike screenplay format, which is rigid because it feeds a production budget, stage play format exists to be read: by a literary manager with a stack of two hundred scripts, by a director planning a rehearsal, by an actor finding their next cue. Every rule below serves one of those readers.

Title page

The title page carries four things and nothing else:

  • The title, centred in capitals, roughly a third of the way down the page.
  • The form, one line below: a play in one act, a play in two acts, a monologue.
  • by on its own line, then your name.
  • Contact details in the lower right: name, email, and optionally phone and agent. If the play is registered or copyrighted, a single line here is enough.

No draft number, no date, no logline, no artwork. Competitions that read blind will ask you to remove the contact block entirely, so keep it in one place where it is easy to strip out.

Character list

The second page is the dramatis personae, headed CHARACTERS. List every speaking character in capitals, in order of appearance or in order of importance, with a one-line description:

                        CHARACTERS

ALEX       30s. The one who stayed.
JORDAN     30s. The one who left.

The description is for casting and for the reader's first impression. Age range, role in the story and one defining trait. Do not describe appearance unless the plot depends on it, and do not write a biography. If a character has no lines, list them anyway with the note (non-speaking).

Below the list, two short italic notes: Setting (where the play takes place, in one sentence) and Time (when). If the play has one location and takes place in real time, say so. Directors read this page before anything else.

Act and scene headings

Act headings are centred, in capitals, numbered in words: ACT ONE, ACT TWO. Each act starts on a new page. Scene headings are centred in title case below them: Scene One, Scene Two. Scenes do not need a page break.

Words, not digits. Act 1, Scene 3 reads as a screenplay habit. A one-act play with no scene divisions can skip headings entirely, but most readers still like a single ACT ONE at the top so they know the form at a glance.

If you give a scene a name, it goes after an em dash: Scene Two — The Return. Keep names to a few words. They are for the reader and the rehearsal schedule, not the audience.

Setting lines

Every scene opens with an italic setting paragraph, set at the left margin, describing what the audience sees and hears when the lights come up:

  A kitchen in a small flat. Early morning. A table,
  two chairs, a window looking onto the street. ALEX
  stands at the window.

This is the one place where a little description is welcome. Give the designer the essentials: place, time of day, season if it matters, the furniture the action needs, and who is on stage at the top. Leave out colour schemes, wallpaper and anything the audience cannot see. If the setting is the same as the previous scene, say The same. That evening. and move on.

Character names and dialogue

The speaking character's name is centred, in capitals, on its own line. The dialogue sits below it, indented from the left margin, in normal sentence case. The name and the first line of dialogue should never be separated by a page break.

                          ALEX
          Four o'clock. They'll be here any minute.

                         JORDAN
          You've rearranged the table three times.

When the same character speaks again after a stage direction, mark it ALEX (cont.) so an actor skimming for their lines does not mistake it for a new speech. When two characters speak at once, put the names on one line joined by an ampersand: ALEX & JORDAN.

Dialogue is the one element with no formatting decisions to make. If you find yourself bolding a word for emphasis, the line needs rewriting, not formatting.

Stage directions and parentheticals

Stage play format has two kinds of direction, and keeping them apart is most of what separates a clean script from a messy one.

Stage directions

Physical action between speeches. Set in italic, in parentheses, indented to the dialogue margin or a little further:

          (JORDAN puts the suitcase down and does not
          sit.)

Write what happens, not what a character feels. (JORDAN hesitates) is a direction. (JORDAN, overwhelmed by the memory of the last time they stood in this kitchen, hesitates) is a novel. The actor and director supply the inside. Present tense, always.

Parentheticals

A short note on delivery, in parentheses, between the character name and the line:

                         JORDAN
                        (quietly)
          I'm not staying.

Use these sparingly. If the line only makes sense delivered one way, the parenthetical is doing work the dialogue should do. Most produced scripts have fewer than one per page.

Cues: entrances, exits, sound and light

Cues are stage directions that stage management will pull out and put on a cue sheet, so they need to be findable. Three rules:

  • Capitalise the character's name on every entrance and exit: (ALEX exits through the door to the street.)
  • Give each cue its own line. Do not bury an entrance inside a paragraph of business.
  • Sound and light cues are plain stage directions: (Lights fade.), (A train passes, close.) You do not need the terms SFX or LX in a submission draft; the production will add them.

A useful check before you send a draft: read only the cues and nothing else. Every character who exits should have entered. Every character on stage at the end of a scene should have been placed there by the setting line or an entrance. This is tedious by hand, which is why The Scriptwriter tracks entrances and exits per scene and flags a character who speaks without being on stage.

Font, margins and page numbers

ElementStandard
Font12-point Courier or Courier New. Some UK publishers accept Times; Courier is never wrong.
PaperUS Letter in North America, A4 in the UK and Europe. Match the theatre you are submitting to.
MarginsOne inch all round, with 1.5 inches on the left if the script will be bound.
Line spacingSingle within a speech. One blank line between elements.
Page numbersTop right. Start counting from the first page of the script. Title and character pages are unnumbered.
Page lengthA page of dialogue runs about a minute on stage. A full-length play is 80 to 120 pages; a one-act, 10 to 40.

The page-a-minute rule only holds if the format is standard. Shrink the font or the margins to make a play look shorter and every reader will know, because the timing will be wrong in the first table read.

US and UK conventions

Everything above is the US standard, derived from the Dramatists Guild's recommended layout and used by most American theatres and competitions. The UK has a parallel tradition, seen in published acting editions from Samuel French and Nick Hern Books, with two visible differences:

  • Character names in the left margin, in capitals, with the dialogue running alongside in a column to the right, instead of centred above the speech.
  • Stage directions in italic without parentheses, often in a narrower column than the dialogue.

Neither is more correct. A UK theatre will read a US-format script without comment and vice versa. What matters is consistency within the script and following any explicit guidelines a theatre or competition publishes. If they publish none, use the format from this guide.

Common mistakes

The ones that appear in nearly every unsolicited submission, in rough order of how often they appear:

  1. Screenplay format. INT. KITCHEN — DAY sluglines, CUT TO transitions, camera directions. A stage play has no camera. If you wrote a first draft in screenwriting software, strip these out before anyone sees it.
  2. Novelistic stage directions. Thoughts, memories, backstory and adverbs. If an actor cannot do it, it is not a stage direction.
  3. Inconsistent character names. ALEX on one page, Alex on the next, ALEXANDER in Act Two. Pick one and use it everywhere, including the character list.
  4. Missing entrances. A character speaks in a scene the setting line did not place them in and no cue brought them on. Readers notice this more than any formatting error because it breaks the picture in their head.
  5. Digits for acts and scenes. Act 2, Scene 1. Use words.
  6. A parenthetical on every line. If every speech needs a delivery note, the dialogue is not carrying its weight.
  7. Non-standard page setup to hit a page count. Everyone can tell.

Get the format right once, in a template, and you never have to think about it again. That is the whole point: the format should disappear so the play is the only thing on the page.
